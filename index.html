Why we're building this

Tonight, in a district hospital with no laboratory, an infection will quietly become sepsis. The blood sample will travel hours to reach a city lab. The result will come back in days. The window that mattered closed on the first one.

The science to catch sepsis early already exists. It just lives inside buildings most people will never reach — central labs, trained technicians, equipment that costs more than the clinic around it.

We think that's an engineering problem, not a medical one. So we're shrinking the lab down to a strip of plastic a nurse can use at the bedside, in a town with no pathologist, on the day it matters.

What it is

A laboratory,
reduced to one strip.

A disposable cartridge and a pocket-sized reader. Nothing to align, nothing to calibrate, nothing to ship to a city.

One drop

About 20 microlitres of whole blood — a finger-prick. Capillary action pulls it through the channel on its own. No pumps, no pipettes, no power for the fluidics.

Fifteen minutes

Fast enough to change what happens next in the same visit — inside the window where antibiotics and escalation still change the outcome.

Two answers

IL-6 rises within hours of infection — the early warning. Procalcitonin separates bacterial from viral, so antibiotics go to the patients who need them.

Where we are

Twelve months from chemistry to a working prototype.

We're early, and we'd rather say so plainly. Here's the path, and exactly where the work stands today.

Months 1–3 · In progress

Assay chemistry

Getting the capture antibodies to bind reliably on screen-printed electrodes.

Months 4–6

Fluidics

Shaping the channel so every sample travels at the same speed, every time.

Months 7–9

The reader

A low-noise board that can resolve currents in the nanoampere range.

Months 10–12

Bench testing

Spiked plasma samples, measured against laboratory reference methods.
